Back to all case studies Case study 1, Amazon

Applying product thinking to Amazon's Recommendation Product Bundle

Don't jump to a solution before understanding the problem.

Scenario

I took the role of a product manager at Amazon, working on the Recommendation Product Bundle (RPB) vertical.

Users in Tier 1 cities had been onboarded successfully, but they were not engaging with RPB during their shopping journey. That lack of engagement was directly contributing to a drop in Average Order Value (AOV) and ARPU.

Objective

Identify product opportunities that increase RPB adoption and improve AOV, using structured product thinking.

What this case study covers

  1. JTBD framework
  2. First-principles thinking
  3. User review analysis
  4. Product opportunity
01

Apply the JTBD framework

02

First-principles thinking

  1. The user's primary intent is to buy the main product.

    They arrived on the page for the laptop, not necessarily for accessories. Any bundle that distracts from the main purchase or adds friction risks being ignored.

  2. The user wants the bundle to offer better value than buying separately.

    If a bundle simply adds up individual prices, without clear savings or visible curation value, the user has no reason to commit upfront.

  3. The user decides whether to add or skip the bundle.

    If evaluating the bundle takes too much effort, such as checking compatibility, comparing prices or assessing unfamiliar products, the user may simply skip it.

03

Summary of user review analysis

Tier 1 shoppers in India have been onboarded to RPB, but they don't use it. They aren't rejecting bundles as an idea. They ignore them because the bundles don't do a job they need at the moment they see them.

Evidence and market signals

Speed over savings

Tier 1 users care more about speed than savings. According to PwC, 50% prioritise fast delivery, while smaller cities prioritise deals.

Quick commerce dominance

Quick commerce has taken over “complete my basket.” Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart now make up about 17% of online retail and about 70% of online grocery, mostly in metros.

Low trust and dark patterns

Indian shoppers are sensitive to dark patterns: 75% report drip pricing and 21% report items sneaked into their basket (LocalCircles). They extend that suspicion to add-ons they didn't ask for.

Ignored rather than disliked

Public complaints about bundles are almost nonexistent. Complaints target ads, delivery and hidden costs instead, so RPB is being ignored rather than disliked.

Customer-facing problems

Trust and relevance

“Are these recommendations actually right for me?”

  • Irrelevant products
  • Sponsored-looking recommendations
  • Untrusted brands
Value and convenience

“Is buying this bundle actually worth it?”

  • Unclear savings
  • Delivery friction
  • Return uncertainty
Discoverability and effort

“Can I find what I need without extra effort?”

  • Page clutter
  • Low visibility
  • Faster alternatives
04

Product opportunities

Opportunity 1, cart enhancement

“Did you forget?” smart cart

Problem. Customers may realise they forgot complementary items, but going back to search for them creates extra effort.

Concept. When a customer adds a product, suggest useful complementary items such as batteries, cables, refills or compatible accessories. The customer can add an item without restarting the shopping journey.

Opportunity 2, goal-oriented AI

AI kit builder

Problem. Customers may have a specific goal but not know which products they actually need. Generic “frequently bought together” recommendations may not match that goal.

Concept. Let customers describe what they want to accomplish, and AI creates a relevant product kit.

For example

“Set up a home office under ₹25,000.” The AI could return a monitor, keyboard, mouse, webcam and desk accessories, along with why each item is recommended, its compatibility and its price.

Zomato: driving engagement and retention through problem framing and prioritization

Moving from understanding user problems to making product decisions: turning research into clear problem statements, exploring multiple solutions, and prioritizing opportunities by user value and business impact.

Scenario

I took the role of a product manager at Zomato, focused on improving user engagement and retention by building new user-facing features.

Objective

Identify user pain points, define the key problems, and prioritize features that can meaningfully drive engagement and retention.

What this case study covers

  1. User research
  2. Personas and problem definition
  3. Solution generation
  4. Prioritization
01

User research

Based on 21 user responses, I identified recurring behavioural patterns and used them to develop directional personas. These personas are hypotheses that would need validation with a larger and more diverse sample.

View the user research survey (opens in a new tab)
02

Personas and problem definition

Persona 1

The value-conscious optimizer

Age
18–34
Location
Urban cities in the research sample
Primary need
Value and convenience

Goals

  • Get good-quality food at a reasonable price
  • Find meaningful discounts and offers
  • Avoid unnecessary additional charges
  • Get better value from repeat orders

Pain points

  • High overall order cost
  • Delivery and additional charges
  • Discounts that don't always feel valuable
  • Price differences across food delivery platforms

Behaviour flow

  1. Craving or need
  2. Browse
  3. Compare
  4. Check offers
  5. Evaluate value
  6. Order

Before ordering, they often ask: “Is this really worth the price I'm paying?”

Persona 2

The experience-driven convenience seeker

Age
18–34
Location
Urban cities in the research sample
Primary need
Convenience, quality and reliability

Goals

  • Quickly find something they want to eat
  • Receive good-quality food
  • Know when their order will arrive
  • Have a smooth experience when something goes wrong

Pain points

  • Long or unpredictable delivery times
  • Poor order tracking or communication
  • Food arriving cold or poorly packaged
  • Inconsistent food quality

Behaviour flow

  1. Craving or need
  2. Browse or search
  3. Evaluate
  4. Order
  5. Track
  6. Experience
  7. Decide to repeat

Before ordering, they often ask: “Can I trust Zomato to give me a smooth experience?”

03

Solution generation

For persona 1, the value-conscious optimizer

1.1

Favourites and smart deal alerts

Users save their favourite restaurants and dishes. Instead of generic promotional notifications, Zomato sends an alert when something relevant changes, including when a favourite restaurant launches a meaningful offer.

Example notification

“Your favourite Butter Chicken is ₹80 cheaper today. Order from your saved restaurant and save ₹80.”

User value. Gives users a reason to return when something they already like becomes better value.

1.2

Value comparison

When users are deciding between restaurants, Zomato helps them compare overall value, not just ratings or food prices.

Restaurant ARestaurant B
Food₹280₹230
Additional charges₹40₹30
Total₹320₹260
Rating4.54.3
Delivery time30 min38 min

Best value: Restaurant B, saving ₹60 with comparable overall satisfaction. Best for speed: Restaurant A, arriving 8 minutes sooner.

User value. Reduces the effort of comparing alternatives and builds confidence in the final choice by making the trade-offs between price, quality and convenience transparent.

1.3

Budget guard

Users set a preferred order budget, for example ₹300. As items and charges are added, Zomato shows the running total against that budget.

Example alert

“Your order is ₹335, which is ₹35 above your ₹300 budget.” To stay within ₹300, Zomato suggests a similar dish (₹245), a better-value combo (₹270) or an alternative restaurant (₹285).

User value. Helps users control spending without forcing them to give up their original craving.

1.4

Value journey

Zomato shows users the cumulative value they have gained from making better decisions over time.

Example monthly summary

7 orders, ₹2,340 spent, ₹380 saved, 5 orders within budget. “Your best-value order this month saved you ₹65 compared with your usual spend.”

User value. Makes accumulated value visible and reinforces the sense that Zomato helps users spend smarter.

For persona 2, the experience-driven convenience seeker

2.1

Food quality rating

Show a separate food quality rating alongside the restaurant's existing overall rating. The overall rating reflects the whole experience, including delivery speed, packaging and the rider. The food quality rating focuses only on the taste and condition of the food received.

Example

Paradise Biryani: 4.4 overall, 4.6 food quality. Restaurant A (4.4 overall, 4.7 food) compared with Restaurant B (4.6 overall, 4.1 food) shows why the split matters.

User value. Helps users judge food quality without reading through multiple reviews.

2.2

Restaurant demand status

When a restaurant is experiencing unusually high demand, Zomato tells the customer proactively.

Before ordering

“High demand right now. This restaurant is receiving more orders than usual, so preparation may take longer.”

After ordering

“Restaurant is busy. Your order is still being prepared while the restaurant handles high order volume.”

User value. Instead of wondering why an order is taking longer, users understand the operational reason behind the delay.

2.3

Live delivery context

Enhance the existing live order map with traffic information along the delivery route, marked as light, moderate or heavy.

Example alert

“Heavy traffic ahead. Estimated delivery time may increase by 8–12 minutes due to local congestion.”

User value. Gives users context when the delivery partner is delayed, instead of leaving them to feel the order has stopped moving without explanation.

04

Prioritization with RICE

I scored each feature on Reach, Impact, Confidence and Effort. Food quality rating came out as the top bet.

RICE score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort

Yummi Plan Smart

Back to all case studies Case study 4, Yummi Plan Smart

Designing a smart meal planning experience for busy lifestyles

Defining the user and the problem, mapping key user flows, building a working prototype in Lovable, and refining the experience through real user feedback.

Scenario

I designed a meal planning tool for busy people who want to organise their week's meals faster and more intelligently. The prototype helps people plan meals based on:

  • Time available to cook
  • Dietary preferences or restrictions
  • Ingredients already in their kitchen

The brief was to define the right problems to solve, map the key flows, and build a working prototype in Lovable, refined with real feedback.

Objective

Make weekly meal planning less stressful and time-consuming, personalised to diet, time and pantry items, and easy to adjust, reuse and track.

What this case study covers

  1. Define the user and problem
  2. Map key user flows
  3. Build the prototype
  4. Test and refine
01

Define the target user and problem

The impact: unhealthy last-minute food choices, wasted groceries, higher expenses and a frustrating weekly planning routine.

Persona 1

Sneha, the busy professional

Location
Bangalore
Household
Lives alone
Diet
Vegetarian

Sneha works long hours and is often too tired to think about what to cook after work. She tries to eat healthily, but last-minute decisions usually push her towards ordering food online.

Goals

  • Cook faster weekday meals, under 30 minutes
  • Order food online less often
  • Avoid buying ingredients she won't use

Pain points

  • Struggles with daily meal decisions and recipe overload
  • Limited cooking time, 25–30 minutes at most on weekdays
  • Forgets to check which ingredients are already in the pantry
Persona 2

Rahul, the fitness-focused meal planner

Location
Mumbai
Household
Lives alone
Diet
High-protein, non-vegetarian

Rahul is a dedicated fitness enthusiast who preps his meals every Sunday to stay on track. He values consistency in his nutrition but wants variety in flavour to keep meals interesting, and looks for quick recipes that fit his schedule and fitness goals.

Goals

  • High-protein meals with simple, accessible ingredients
  • Weekly batch planning that fits his routine
  • Avoid eating out, to stay consistent with fitness

Pain points

  • Hard to find recipes that match exact macro and protein goals
  • Gets bored repeating the same meals every day
  • No time to search for new recipes daily
02

Map the key user flows

The end-to-end flow covers user actions, system processes and decision points across four stages.

User actionSystem processDecision

Onboarding

  1. User opens the app
  2. Welcome screen, taps “Get Started”
  3. Selects dietary preferences
  4. Sets available cooking time
  5. Adds pantry items
  6. Chooses meals per day and days to plan
  7. Confirm setup?

    No Return to the inputs and adjust

    Yes Profile is created

  8. Redirect to the home dashboard

Plan generation and review

  1. User taps “Generate Weekly Plan”
  2. System gathers inputs: diet, cooking time, pantry items, days and meals
  3. System filters recipes by dietary match, time match and pantry match
  4. System creates a 7-day plan and shows it to the user
  5. Happy with the plan?

    No Edit the plan: swap a meal, drag and drop, or replace with a faster recipe, then return to the plan view

    Yes Save and finalise the weekly plan

Shopping list

  1. User taps “Create Shopping List”
  2. System subtracts pantry items and groups the rest by category
  3. User reviews the list
  4. Make changes?

    Yes Edit quantities or items, then return to the list

    No Shop and mark items as bought

Cooking

  1. During the week, user opens a recipe and enters Cook Mode
  2. Step-by-step instructions with timers
  3. User marks ingredients as used
  4. Pantry updates automatically
  5. End
03

Build the prototype in Lovable

I built and deployed the prototype in Lovable, bringing the mapped flows to life with a clear interface, a responsive meal calendar and instant recipe generation.

Open the prototype (Lovable) (opens in a new tab)
04

Test and refine

Feedback from real users

  • Users felt the app already included all the core features needed for daily meal planning.
  • There was strong interest in using it as a real mobile app.
  • The most requested enhancement was a nutritional auto-calculator. Users wanted to see the calories in each food item and its health benefits, such as sustained energy, muscle repair, digestion and immunity.
  • This feature significantly increased perceived value. Users said they would be willing to pay for the app if it were included.

Refinement and impact

Problem

Users lacked clear visibility into the nutritional value, calorie count and health benefits of food items, which made informed choices harder.

Solution

I added a nutritional auto-calculator to the prototype, showing calories and key health benefits for each meal item.

Impact

The experience became more informative and value-driven, helping users make better food decisions and increasing engagement and willingness to pay.

02

Visualise the core flows

Two features, each mapped end to end.

Feature 1: Buddy Streaks with lightweight chat

  1. User opens the home screen
  2. Taps “Start Buddy Streak”
  3. Selects 1–3 friends and sends invites
  4. Do all friends accept?

    No Wait for pending invites

    Yes Continue

  5. When all buddies complete at least one workout on the same day, the Buddy Streak starts (Day 1)
  6. User opens the app and views the streak status (Day X)
  7. Does the user complete a workout?

    Yes Update streak progress and show positive reinforcement

    No Check the time window

  8. If any buddy is incomplete, the system marks the streak “at risk” and notifies the others, who can send a nudge or message
  9. Does the pending buddy complete a workout?

    Yes Streak saved and maintained

    No Use a streak saver if one is available. If not, the streak breaks and chat is disabled

  10. Lightweight chat stays active: short messages and emojis, with automatic streak updates
  11. Loop continues

Feature 2: Trainer Circles

  1. User attends a trainer's class
  2. Views the trainer's profile and taps “Join Trainer Circle”
  3. Does the circle have open slots? (maximum 30–50)

    No User joins a waitlist and is notified when a slot opens

    Yes User joins the circle and the member count updates

  4. User opens the Trainer Circle in the app
  5. Views trainer posts: tips, motivation and challenges
  6. Reactions and comments are recorded as interactions
  7. If the trainer posts a challenge: the user joins, completes workouts, progress is tracked and completion is acknowledged
  8. If a live class is scheduled: the user joins, the trainer sees member context such as a 5-day streak, and gives a name-based shoutout
  9. Retention loop continues
